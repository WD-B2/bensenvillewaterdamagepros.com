How to Find a Hidden Water Leak in Your Bensenville Home

Two household tests and a short list of warning signs can tell you whether water is escaping before a ceiling or floor shows it.

By the Bensenville Water Damage Pros team

(630) 580-6401

A hidden water leak is water escaping from a supply line, drain, appliance connection or roof where you cannot see it, often inside a wall, ceiling or floor. Many leaks announce themselves late, after a stain spreads or a bill jumps. You can run two simple tests at home, learn the physical signs and know when moisture meters and thermal imaging are worth bringing in.

Start with the water meter test

The meter test checks whether water is moving through your service line when nothing should be using it. It works for a leak anywhere between the meter and your fixtures.

Do this when the household can stay out of the water for a while.

  1. Turn off every fixture, the dishwasher, the washing machine and the ice maker, and make sure no one flushes a toilet.
  2. Find the meter, usually in the basement or utility area near where the line enters, and read the dial or digits.
  3. Look for the small low-flow indicator, a triangle or wheel, and note whether it spins while everything is off.
  4. Wait a good while without using any water, then read the meter again.
  5. If the numbers moved or the indicator kept turning, water is going somewhere and you have a leak to find.

Run a toilet dye test

Toilets are the most common quiet leak because a worn flapper lets water slip into the bowl without a sound. Remove the tank lid, add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and do not flush.

Wait a while. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper or fill valve is leaking. That is a plumbing repair, not a restoration job, and it also helps explain a meter that kept moving during your test.

Physical signs inside the house

Not every leak reaches the meter test, especially drain leaks or roof leaks. Walk through the house and look for patterns you could dismiss one at a time but that mean more together.

Keep a short log for a week. Note the date, the room and what changed, such as a stain that grew after laundry day. Patterns in that log give a plumber or a moisture technician a place to start, and they cost you nothing but a notepad.

  • A stain or ring that grows or darkens after a shower, a dishwasher cycle or rain.
  • Warped or lifting baseboard, cupped flooring or paint that bubbles on a wall.
  • A musty smell in a closet, cabinet or room that never fully airs out.
  • The faint sound of trickling when every tap is off.
  • A water bill that rises without any change in how the household uses water.
  • A floor that feels cool or damp in one spot over a slab.

Bensenville water service and your meter

Bensenville's drinking water starts as Lake Michigan source water, is treated by the City of Chicago, is conveyed by the DuPage Water Commission and is distributed by the Village's Public Works Utilities Division. That chain ends at your meter. For a question about your meter, its reading or a bill that does not match your use, call Public Works at (630) 350-3435 before you assume the leak is inside your house.

The Village side stops at the meter, and what happens behind it is yours or your landlord's to handle. With about half of Bensenville homes rented, per the CMAP housing toolkit, tenants should report a suspected leak to the landlord in writing. Neighbors in Schiller Park and Franklin Park run the same meter test.

When professionals bring better tools

Your eyes and a meter dial have limits. Restoration professionals carry pin and pinless moisture meters that read the wetness of drywall, wood and subfloor. They also use thermal imaging cameras, which show temperature patterns that often track wet areas behind a surface.

These tools find damage, not necessarily the exact pipe. A plumber locates and repairs the leak, and the moisture work documents what became wet so drying can be planned. Treat the two as separate jobs.

What to do once you confirm a leak

If you know where the water is coming from, close the nearest shut-off valve or the main. Take photos before you move anything, and keep receipts for emergency fixes. The Illinois Department of Insurance advises making essential temporary repairs to prevent more damage but not permanent repairs before talking to your insurer.

A slow leak can leave materials damp for weeks. Ask the professional you hire whether they will take moisture readings and share them in writing, so you know the wall is dry before it is closed.

If the meter test and the signs both point to a leak and you still cannot locate it, stop opening walls on your own. Cutting into a wet wall near wiring, or removing a section of ceiling that is holding water, can make the situation worse.

Questions about this topic

Can I do the water meter test if my meter is outside?

Yes. Many meters are in a basement, but some sit in a pit or box outside. Use the same method and take care with lids and winter conditions.

If you cannot find or read it, call Public Works at the number the Village lists.

Who should I call for a water meter question in Bensenville?

Public Works, which includes the Utilities Division, is the Village contact at (630) 350-3435.

They can speak to meter readings and billing questions, while a plumber handles piping inside the home.

My meter shows a leak but I can see nothing wet. What now?

Check the toilet with dye, outdoor hose bibs and any irrigation line first. If the meter still moves, a plumber can pressure-test or locate the line.

Ask a restoration company to check nearby walls and floors for moisture once the plumber has an idea of the area.

Does a higher water bill mean I have a leak?

Not always. Guests, a new appliance or seasonal watering can raise use. A bill that climbs with no change in habits is a good reason to run the meter test.

Compare several months rather than one.

Will my insurance cover a hidden leak?

In general terms, sudden and accidental damage is often covered and slow, long-running leaks often are not. Policies differ, so check yours and ask your insurer.

Document everything and report promptly once you decide to file.
